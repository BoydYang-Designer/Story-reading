Let's talk about failure. Every designer has rejected ideas, broken prototypes, and ideas that don't really work out. And the mistake being made here isn't the failures.

It's that you're trying to avoid those failures. In design, failure isn't just inevitable. It's actually quite useful.

Every test that fails, every prototype that breaks, and every idea that your client might hate, isn't just failure. It's actually feedback. It's feedback because iteration and progression is built on small failures.

Small, repeated failures. If nothing you've ever made fails, then you're probably not pushing far enough. You should really stop treating failure as a roadblock or an outcome, and treat it more as data points that you can use to progress and further work on your product.

I mean, just look at James Dyson. He went through 5127 different failed prototypes before he actually made his vacuum work. And the thing is he didn't see each of those prototypes as wasted attempts.

Each one of those attempts taught him something different about his problem. That mindset is what turned failure into progression and ultimately his success. I'm pretty sure you can see this everywhere.

If a chair collapses, then you know the materials or the joints were wrong. If a client rejects your pitch, then you know what kind of questions you should have asked earlier on. User testing destroys your first product, then you know exactly what you need to fix.

Good design hardly ever comes from getting it right on the first try. It comes from learning faster each time and making progress from those failures. So I urge you to rethink what failure means.

It's never going to be a reflection of your ability. It's just going to be a reflection of your progress and your ability to continue progressing forward. The only real failure in design is one you don't learn from.
